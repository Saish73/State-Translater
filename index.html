Introduction to Śāstras

In the Indian intellectual tradition, Śāstra refers to a systematic body of knowledge or a treatise governing a particular domain of life — spiritual, social, legal, scientific, or artistic. The textual foundation of this tradition is layered into several categories. The वेद (Vedas) — Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda — are considered Śruti ("that which is heard"), believed to be eternal and revealed rather than authored by any human being. Below the Vedas sit the Upanishads, philosophical appendices to the Vedas that explore metaphysical questions of self and reality. The Smritis (literally "that which is remembered"), such as the Manusmriti and Yajnavalkya Smriti, are human-authored texts that codify social, ethical, and legal conduct based on the Vedic worldview. The Puranas are narrative and mythological texts that popularize dharmic teachings through stories, while the Dharmasutras are older, aphoristic legal manuals that predate the more elaborate Smriti codes.

Because these texts were composed across centuries by different authors for different audiences, they frequently contain rules that appear to conflict with one another on matters of ritual, marriage, inheritance, diet, and conduct. To resolve such conflicts without discarding any text as invalid, Indian jurisprudence developed a formal doctrine of rule precedence — a hierarchy of authority (प्रामाण्य, prāmāṇya) that determines which text's rule prevails when two sources disagree. This principle, discussed extensively in Mīmāṃsā (the school of Vedic textual interpretation) and later Dharmashastra commentaries, is foundational to how classical Indian legal reasoning (Vyavahāra) actually functioned in practice.

This project reframes that classical doctrine using a concept familiar from computer science: state transition logic. In a rule-based system, when multiple rules could apply to the same input, the system must transition to a single valid state by applying a priority function. Here, each Śāstra type is assigned a fixed precedence value, and the "system" (our simulator) computes which rule wins when two Śāstras are compared — mirroring, in a simplified computational model, the same conflict-resolution logic that ancient Indian scholars used to reconcile centuries of textual tradition.

  • Śruti (Vedas) is considered apaurusheya (not of human origin) and therefore holds the highest authority in matters of dharma.
  • Rule precedence allows apparently contradictory texts to coexist within a single legal-ethical tradition without one being declared "wrong."
  • State transition modeling gives us a modern, computational lens to visualize an ancient system of textual hierarchy and conflict resolution.

Traditional Śāstra Hierarchy (Rule Precedence Order)

In Indian Knowledge Systems, when two rules conflict, the Śāstra with higher authority prevails. This order was formalized by Dharmashastra commentators and the Mīmāṃsā school of exegesis to ensure consistent interpretation across a vast and diverse textual corpus.

Real Examples from Indian Texts

These illustrative cases show how the precedence doctrine has traditionally been applied to resolve conflicts across different domains of dharma — ritual, law, and daily conduct.

1. Marriage Ritual Conflict

Scenario

The Vedic injunction emphasizes a single marriage (ekavivāha), but some Smriti texts permit remarriage under specific conditions, such as the death of a spouse or inability to bear children.

Resolution

The Vedic (Śruti) rule prevails as the general norm, but Smriti provides contextual exceptions for special circumstances, since Smriti derives its authority from — and elaborates upon — Śruti rather than contradicting it.

Source: Manusmriti 9.101; Rigveda 10.85

2. Inheritance Law Dispute

Scenario

The Arthashastra prescribes equal division of property among sons, while certain local customs favor a larger share for the eldest son.

Resolution

In worldly and administrative matters (Vyavahāra), codified state law such as the Arthashastra generally prevails over uncodified local custom, though regional practice was often accommodated in practice.

Source: Arthashastra 3.1; Yajnavalkya Smriti 2.136

3. Dietary Rules Variation

Scenario

Puranic literature broadly recommends vegetarianism as an ideal of non-violence (ahimsa), while certain Vedic ritual texts describe meat offerings within the context of yajna (sacrifice).

Resolution

Vedic injunctions in this case are treated as context-specific — applying narrowly to ritual performance — while Puranic guidance governs general daily conduct, so the two are read as complementary rather than contradictory.

Source: Shatapatha Brahmana; Bhagavata Purana 11.5.14
